CHAPTER 01
Kill complacency
Five years in, the money we lose comes from habit and assumption.
$23K → $37K
A premium US contractor lifted its average ticket 62% by changing how it sold, not what it sold.
The goal
Sharpen the edges. Nobody here needs the basics, so today is about the last ten per cent.
The enemy
Autopilot. Same pitch, same options, same guess at what the customer can spend.
Today
Find the money we leave on the table every week, and take it back.
Source: Momnt case study, Braga Brothers (US)

CHAPTER 02
Stop prejudging affordability
Never decide the price in your head before the customer sees it.
57%
of Americans have under $1,000 in savings. You cannot see wealth, or the lack of it, from the driveway.
The trap
A modest house or a worried face triggers a mental discount. You're guessing with your own wallet.
The reality
The tidy older home may have no mortgage. The flashy one may be drowning in debt.
The rule
Present Good, Better, Best every time. No editing, no assuming. Their choice reveals the budget.
Source: Synchrony data via ACCA (US)

CHAPTER 03
Read the trust signals
Present after the shift, not before.
81%
of consumers say they must trust a brand to do what is right before they will buy from it.
Forward questions
They stop asking defensive questions and start asking: how long will it last, when can you start?
Open body language
They relax, lean in and stop guarding. The conversation feels easier.
New problems
They start telling you about other issues around the house. That's trust.
Source: Edelman Trust Barometer Special Report, 2019 (8 countries, 16,000 people)

CHAPTER 05
Build options around what they want
One problem, not a list of jobs.
84% vs 32%
picked the premium option when a weaker option sat beside it, versus when it didn't. The line-up changes the choice.
Frame the system
An ageing system failing in stages. Patching one part just moves the leak to the next weak point.
Whole job or small first?
High trust: go for the whole thing. Low trust: earn it with one great job. Never go small out of fear of the number.
Their wants, not ours
Options built around what the customer told you matters, not what we'd like to sell.
Source: Dan Ariely, The Economist subscription experiment

CHAPTER 07
Anchor and present the big number
Make doing it properly once feel like the obvious choice.
25% vs 45%
A random number shifted people's estimates by 20 points. The first figure they hear sets the scale.
~2x
Losses feel about twice as heavy as equal gains. The cost of failure outweighs the price.
Anchor honestly
Walk through total failure: the 2:00am burst, water through the ceiling, the damage bill. Consequences, not scare tactics.
Name the hero
The Complete Renewal sits at the top. The lesser options are visibly compromises.
Let them choose up
People want to be the one who says: I'd rather just do it properly once.
Sources: Tversky & Kahneman, 1974; meta-analysis of 607 loss aversion estimates, 2024

CHAPTER 08
Go quiet
Scope. Price. Silence.
2.1 sec
Top sellers pause about two seconds after stating price, across 519,000 recorded sales calls.
5x
Star reps pause five times longer after an objection. Average reps talk faster.
Don't justify
The silence feels unbearable to you, not to them.
Don't discount
Never drop the price to ease your own discomfort.
Let them fill it
Whoever speaks next should be the customer.
Source: Gong Labs call analysis

CHAPTER 10
Who actually wants finance
Finance means savvy, not broke.
65%
of Americans have under $1,000 set aside for home repairs. Lenders target these same three customer types.
Cash is king
Often the wealthiest. Could pay outright, but would rather keep money invested or liquid.
Cashflow smoother
A solid earner who doesn't want a 30 grand hit in one month.
Budget constrained
Genuinely couldn't do the job otherwise. Finance is why the sale happens.
Source: Synchrony data via ACCA (US)

CHAPTER 10
How to promote finance
Make it standard, not special.
7% → 80%
Financed share of deals once one contractor led with finance on every quote. Average ticket rose 62%.
Offer it to everyone
Casually, on every big job. Offered only to the person who looks like they need it, it becomes shameful.
Give a flattering reason
“A lot of our customers prefer to keep their cash free and just spread this out.”
Before the objection
Offered upfront, it's a feature. Offered after “too expensive”, it's a lifeline.
Source: Momnt case study, Braga Brothers (US)
