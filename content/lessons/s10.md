## Work orders and approval limits

Property manager jobs usually start with a work order. Read it before you arrive: what's been reported, the approval limit, who to contact, and how access is arranged.

**The approval limit is the most the property manager has approved without being asked again.** Limits vary between agencies and properties. [CONFIRM: typical PM approval limits]

**Never exceed the PM's approval limit without written approval.** Not on a verbal OK, and not because the tenant says the agent won't mind.

When the fix costs more than the limit:

1. **Make it safe and explain to the tenant** that the property manager has to approve anything over their limit.
2. **Send the PM photos and the options**, Elite first, so they can decide quickly. Don't sit and wait for them to ring you.
3. **Document the approval before you go ahead.** Get it in writing and save it to the job in ServiceTitan.

Don't squeeze in a cheaper fix that fits under the limit but won't actually solve the problem, and never ask the tenant to pay or approve.

Property managers on account follow their account terms for payment. [CONFIRM: which customers are on account terms]

> **On the job:** The hot water has failed at a rental in Merrylands. The work order says the PM's approval limit is $300, and the fix costs more than that. The tenant says, "Just fix it. The agent won't mind." What do you do, and what do you say to the tenant?

## Tenant access and notice

On a rental, **the property manager approves the work and the tenant gives access.** Tenants aren't the decision-maker, however friendly or insistent they are.

Getting in:

- **Check the access details** on the work order before you go: who'll be home, keys, pets and parking.
- **Call when you're on the way**, like any job.
- **If the access arrangements fall through** (nobody's home, or the keys aren't where they should be), call Kim (residential dispatch) rather than improvise.

Notice: tenants have rights about when someone can enter their home for repairs, and the rules differ for urgent and non-urgent work. Giving notice is the landlord's or agent's job, but it's why you arrive in the agreed window. [VERIFY: NSW notice rules for entering a rented home for repairs, including urgent repairs]

Inside, it's the same standard as any home: shoe covers on, drop sheets down, and leave it cleaner than you found it.

- **Be friendly, but don't agree extra work with the tenant.** If they point out another problem, photograph it and pass it to the PM.
- **Don't discuss the landlord, the rent or who's at fault.** Stick to what you found.

> **Say it like this:** "Happy to have a look at that while I'm here. I'll photograph it and send it to your property manager, because they're the one who approves the work."

## Landlords: protecting the asset

**With a landlord, the focus is protecting the asset**: avoiding emergency call-outs, preventing damage, and keeping the tenant happy. Sometimes you deal with them through the PM, and sometimes directly.

Frame what you find in those terms:

- **The cost of doing nothing.** A perished flexi hose in a rental can mean a flood, an after-hours call-out and an unhappy tenant. That's a consequence, not a scare tactic.
- **The hot water age flag.** Units around 8 years or older get flagged for follow-up.
- **Photos and plain facts.** Many landlords never see the property. Your photos are their eyes.

The **Landlord Compliance Pack** is built for this:

- **Essential**: fix the tenant's reported issue.
- **Standard**: plus a leak check of all fixtures.
- **Premium**: plus flexi hose replacement and a water-efficiency check for tenant water billing. [VERIFY: current NSW water-efficiency requirements for tenant water billing]
- **Elite**: plus water-efficient tapware and shower roses throughout, a hot water condition report, and an annual Care Plan for the property.

Your notes can become a Real Estate report, so record observations, not legal opinions. Never write "illegal" or "non-compliant".

> **On the job:** Fixing a tenant's dripping shower in Granville, you find rusting flexi hoses under every sink. You photograph each one and send the PM the options. The landlord lives interstate and has never seen those hoses. Your photos are what they decide on.

## Strata: common property vs lot

A strata building is run by a strata manager and a committee on behalf of the owners. **Always record whether the problem is on common property or in a lot**, because it changes who approves and who pays.

What counts as common property and what belongs to a lot depends on the building's strata plan and NSW strata law, and some cases are genuinely grey. [VERIFY: how NSW strata law and the strata plan decide common property vs lot for plumbing] It's not your call to make on site.

Your job is the facts:

- **Where the problem is**: which lot, which room, which pipe, and where it appears to start.
- **What the pipe serves**: one lot, or several?
- **Photos and video** that show the location clearly, numbered and referred to in your notes.
- **Observations, not opinions.** "Leak appears to come from the shared stack in the ceiling space (photos 3 to 5)" is useful. "Strata will have to pay for this" isn't yours to say.

Larger strata work goes to Bella. That's the next lesson.

> **On the job:** Water is coming through the bathroom ceiling of a unit in Zetland. The tenant is sure it's the unit upstairs. It could be their shower, or a shared pipe in the ceiling. You photograph everything, record exactly where it's coming through, and note that it needs checking from above. You don't guess who pays.

How to pass it on:

2. **Write it for someone who wasn't there**: where it is, what you saw, who you spoke to and how to reach them.

> **On the job:** You're clearing a blocked sink in a Parramatta café. The owner mentions the grease trap keeps overflowing, and that the landlord owns the two shops next door. That's commercial work. Photograph it, note the details, and raise it for Alex. Don't quote it from the counter.
