## The efficiency model: estimated vs actual hours

Every job is estimated to take a number of hours, built from the pricebook and the scope. That estimate is your benchmark.

**Your pay is driven by the gap between estimated and actual hours**, with the quality fully intact.

A simple example. A job is estimated at four hours, and two installers both do it perfectly:

- The first takes the full four hours. Good work, but no extra value.
- The second takes three, to exactly the same standard, because they prepped the night before, carried the right stock and worked in a clean sequence. They created an hour of value.

The estimate is a target to beat. It isn't a cap to stretch the work to fill, and it isn't a licence to rush (lesson 4 covers why).

Two things keep the model fair:

- **Real-time timesheets.** Your hours are measured from what's recorded in ServiceTitan. Back-fill them at the end of the week and the hours you saved disappear.
- **Honest feedback.** If a task keeps taking much longer or shorter than its estimate, tell the Install Manager, so the pricebook can be fixed.

> **On the job:** A re-pipe in Marsfield is estimated at a full day. You confirmed the parts and access the day before and planned your sequence. You finish an hour early, test everything and leave the site spotless. That hour counts, because the quality held.

## Why every saved hour is worth about $50

**Every hour you save against the estimate is worth about $50.**

Here's where that comes from. The fixed price assumed a certain number of labour hours. Deliver the same finished result in fewer hours, and the job earns the same revenue for less cost. That saved cost is real money.

Once you think of your day in $50 blocks, the small leaks get obvious:

- A 30-minute supply run you could have avoided is about $25 gone.
- Ten minutes hunting for tools on each of five jobs is nearly an hour: about $50, every day.
- A return trip can wipe out the whole saving on a job.

A worked example: beat the estimate by an hour on three jobs this week, with nothing coming back, and that's about $150 of value.

On top of efficiency, you earn **1%** on extra work you raise that's sold and completed, and **$100 for every genuine 5-star review**. [CONFIRM: exact install pay plan]

Never create a saved hour by skipping a step. That's the next lesson.

> **On the job:** For your sign-off, a lead installer asks you to explain your pay model. Use one of your own jobs from this week: the estimated hours, your actual hours, the gap, what it's worth at about $50 an hour, and whether anything came back.

## Why speed without quality is failure (the lifetime labour warranty)

The fastest installer isn't the best one. **The best installer is fast and right the first time.**

Every customer gets our lifetime labour warranty. [CONFIRM: approved wording, including what's excluded] If our workmanship fails, we go back and fix it, and the customer doesn't pay. So rework always comes back to us.

When a rushed job comes back, the costs land all at once:

- the hour you saved is gone, plus the hours to go back
- the return visit eats the profit, which is only about $200 on a $1,000 job
- a customer who was happy now isn't, and that's not a 5-star review
- your name is on the job

**A warranty return wipes out the time you saved and costs the business its profit on the job.** You went backwards to save an hour.

Real speed comes from preparation, sequence and skill. Fake speed comes from skipping steps: no leak test, a rushed fit-off, no clean-up, materials not recorded. If your speed comes from doing less of the work, it's a return trip waiting to happen.

> **On the job:** You finish a vanity and mixer install in Toongabbie 40 minutes early, but skip the final leak check under the basin. Two weeks later the customer calls about a swollen cabinet. The 40 minutes is gone, the return is unpaid, and a happy customer is now an unhappy one.

## What top installers do differently

Watch the best installers for a week and you won't see superhuman skill. You'll see plain habits, done every day:

- **They're ready the night before.** Van stocked, fuel in, specials arranged, rolling at 7am.
- **They read the job before they arrive.** Scope, parts, access and hours, checked the day before.
- **They beat estimates with clean work.** The right sequence, touch it once, clean as you go.
- **They test everything before they leave.** Pressure, flow and leaks.
- **They document everything.** Before-and-after photos and video, saved to the job.
- **They spot genuine extra work** and raise it with photos.
- **They collect the balance and get sign-off** before they drive away.
- **They keep dispatch and the Install Manager informed**, in real time.

None of it is talent. It's routine, which means anyone can learn it.

Finish each week with five questions. Did I beat my estimates cleanly? Did anything come back? Did I document every job? Did I collect every payment? Did I raise the work I spotted? Five yeses is a winning week.

> **On the job:** Two installers on the same crew both say they're flat out. One has had three returns this month and is always at the supplier. The other is rolling at 7am, rarely goes back and keeps getting mentioned in reviews. The difference isn't skill. It's the list above.
