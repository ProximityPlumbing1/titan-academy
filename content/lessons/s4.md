## The water meter test

The meter test answers one question: **is water escaping somewhere after the meter?** It won't tell you where. It tells you whether there's a leak to find, so run it first on every leak job.

1. Turn off everything that uses water: taps, toilets (let them finish filling), irrigation, pool top-up, dishwasher and washing machine.
2. Photograph the meter reading, including the low-flow indicator if it has one, and note the time.
3. Wait, using no water, long enough for even a small leak to show.
4. Photograph the reading again and note the time.

If the reading has moved, or the low-flow indicator is turning, water is escaping. Narrow it down by isolating one section at a time, like the cold supply to the hot water unit or each toilet's isolation valve, and watching whether the meter stops.

**Leak detection reports can end up with insurers or in disputes, so record precise facts, photos and locations.** Your impression isn't evidence. The meter is.

> **On the job:** A customer in Pennant Hills has a water bill that's suddenly jumped, and there's no sign of a leak anywhere. They want you to "just find it". Where do you start, what do you test, and what do you record before you touch a single wall?

## Detection methods: visual, thermal, acoustic, tracer gas

Start with the simplest method and move to the specialised ones only when you need to. Record what each one showed, **including what it ruled out**.

- **Visual**: stains, bubbling paint, swollen skirting, mould, lifting floors, loose tiles. A moisture meter maps how far the damp has spread. Water travels before it shows, so look around and below the obvious spot.
- **Thermal**: a thermal camera shows temperature differences. A hot water leak shows warm and a cold one can show cool. It points to the likely area but doesn't prove a leak on its own.
- **Acoustic**: listening equipment picks up pressurised water escaping through walls, floors and slabs. It works best in a quiet house.
- **Tracer gas**: the line is filled with a safe tracer gas, and a detector finds where it escapes. It's for leaks the other methods can't pinpoint, like under a slab.

## Plumbing vs waterproofing (AS 3740)

Water through a ceiling, or into the room next to a bathroom, isn't always a plumbing fault. It can be:

- **a plumbing fault**: a leaking pipe or fitting in the wall, the mixer, the shower arm or the waste
- **a waterproofing failure**: the membrane under the tiles has failed. Waterproofing of wet areas is covered by AS 3740.
- **a building defect**: cracked tiles, failed grout or silicone, poor falls, or a structural problem

**Separate them clearly** by testing one thing at a time:

1. Test the supply pipes with the shower off. If the meter holds, the pressurised pipes probably aren't the problem.
2. Run water down the waste only, keeping the walls and floor dry.
3. Then wet the tiled floor and walls separately.

Record which test produced the leak, with photos and times. Write what you saw, not who's to blame. "The builder did a dodgy job" can end up in a dispute.

If it's a membrane problem, say so in your notes and recommend a waterproofing specialist. [VERIFY: current AS 3740 requirements, and the NSW licence needed for waterproofing work]

> **On the job:** A customer in Maroubra is sure a pipe has burst because the hallway carpet is wet. The pipes hold and the waste is fine, but water appears when you wet the shower floor. That's a waterproofing finding, and your notes need to say so.

## Insurance jobs

**Insurance work spikes after storms.** Assume your notes and photos could end up in front of an insurer or an assessor. Your job is the facts. The insurer decides what's covered.

- **Make safe first.** Isolate, contain and protect the home. Photograph the damage before you touch anything, and again afterwards.
- **Record exactly what you found**: where the water came from, what you tested, the meter readings, and photos with locations.
- **Don't guess how long it's been leaking.** Describe what you saw, like staining or rotted timber, and let the assessor decide.
- **Never tell a customer what their insurance will or won't cover.** You haven't read their policy.
- **Never change a finding to help a claim.** An honest record protects the customer, the business and you.

## Strata leaks: lot vs common property

In a strata building, **always record whether the leak is in a lot or on common property.** It changes who approves the work and who pays.

Roughly, a lot is the owner's unit and common property is the shared building, run by the strata manager and committee. Exactly which pipes, walls and membranes belong to which depends on the rules, the strata plan and the by-laws. So don't decide it for them. Record the facts the strata manager needs:

- the unit, the room and exactly where the water is showing
- where it's coming from, as far as you've proved it, including the unit above
- whether the pipe serves one unit or several, like a shared stack
- photos, meter readings and every test you did

Never tell an owner "strata will pay", or a strata manager "that's the owner's problem". It isn't your call.

> **On the job:** In a Randwick unit block, water is coming through a ground-floor bathroom ceiling. It could be the shower in the unit above or the shared stack in the wall. You record both possibilities, what you tested and which one your evidence points to.

## Notes for a Leak Detection report: findings only

Your notes become the customer's Leak Detection report. **The report is findings only. A formal quote follows separately.** It may go to an insurer, a strata manager or into a dispute, so it must read as a record of facts, not a sales document.

Use the standard note structure:

1. **Why called**: what the customer reported, and when they first noticed it.
2. **What I found**: meter readings and times, each method and what it showed, what you ruled out, and the exact location.
3. **What I did**: anything you isolated, made safe or repaired.
4. **What I recommend**: the fix, in plain English, with no prices.

Number your photos and refer to them, for example "photo 4: thermal image, warm area above the shower mixer".

- No prices, options or discounts. They go on the quote.
- No blame and no legal opinions. Never write "illegal" or "non-compliant".
- Keep plumbing faults separate from waterproofing or building defects.

> **On the job:** Your first draft for a Concord leak says "dodgy old pipework, needs a full re-pipe". Rewrite it as findings: "Copper joint behind the kitchen wall leaking under pressure (photos 2 to 5). Recommend repair of the joint. Pipework of a similar age noted throughout."
