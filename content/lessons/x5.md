## Anchor honestly: walk through total failure first (the burst, the ceiling, the damage bill), then the full fix

Every price gets compared to something. If you don't set the comparison, the customer will, and they'll hold your Elite number up against the cheapest patch they can imagine.

So **set it honestly, before the price**. Walk them through what total failure looks like in their home: the 2am burst, the water through the ceiling, the damage bill. Then show the full fix. Against an emergency and a damage bill, doing it once sounds reasonable.

Two well-replicated findings explain why:

- **Anchoring.** In a classic experiment, a random number shifted people's estimates by 20 points (25% vs 45%). **The first figure they hear sets the scale.**
- **Loss aversion.** Losses feel about twice as heavy as equal gains. The cost of failure outweighs the price.

It only works if it's true. **Consequences, not scare tactics.** Describe what could genuinely happen to *this* system, use your photos and video as evidence, and never invent a disaster.

> **On the job:** A 1960s home in Carlingford: galvanised pipe, three leaks this year and low pressure. One partner is keen; the other says "just patch the leaks". Before you say a single number, what do you walk them through?

*Sources: Tversky & Kahneman, 1974, and a 2024 meta-analysis of 607 loss aversion estimates, both cited in Proximity's October 2026 "Selling the Big Job" session. It's global, well-replicated research on how people tend to decide, not a guarantee about any one customer.*

## Elite is the hero: the 'do it properly once' option. Present it first so the options below it are visibly compromises

Every big presentation needs a hero, and it's **Elite: the "do it properly once" option**. It's the best outcome for the home, future-proofed, and built from everything you found.

Present it **first**, so the options below it are **visibly compromises**. Essential still fixes today's problem, and Standard and Premium are solid choices. But next to Elite, the customer can see what each one leaves out: the root cause that's still there, the next weak point, the Care Plan they don't get.

The line-up changes the choice. In one well-known experiment, **84% picked the premium option when a weaker option sat beside it, against 32% when it didn't.** Our lower options aren't decoys. They're real choices, and seeing them side by side makes the trade-offs obvious.

Give the hero a name they'll remember. The October session called it the Complete Renewal. It's still Elite on the quote, but describe it in their terms: "you never think about the plumbing again".

> **Say it like this:** "This is the one that does it properly, once. Everything below it fixes part of the problem, and I'll show you exactly which part."

*Source: Dan Ariely, The Economist subscription experiment, cited in Proximity's October 2026 "Selling the Big Job" session.*

## Elite first, with full confidence ('the option I'd put in my own home'), then narrow to the two that fit this home. Don't read it like a menu

**Lead with Elite, with full confidence**, as your genuine recommendation. Then **narrow to the two options that fit this home**.

Lead with the top option and everything below it sounds reasonable. **Lead with Essential, and everything above it sounds like a rip-off.** Same four options, same prices. Only the order changed.

**Don't read it like a menu.** Four options read top to bottom, line by line, turn a recommendation into homework. Instead:

1. Present Elite first, in the customer's terms.
2. Narrow to the two that fit what they told you in discovery.
3. Keep every option on the page. You still present all four. Narrowing is about where the conversation lives.

Only call it the option you'd put in your own home if it's true. Built honestly from your findings, it should be.

> **Say it like this:** "The option I'd put in my own home is Elite. Let me show you why, and then what sits below it."

> **On the job:** Back at the Carlingford re-pipe, you lead with Elite and the full re-pipe, then narrow: "For how long you're planning to stay, it's really between these two." The patch is still on the page. It just isn't where the conversation lives.

## Let them choose up: people want to be the one who says 'I'd rather just do it properly once'

The best big sales don't feel pushed. The customer **chooses up**, and it's their idea.

People want to be the one who says **"I'd rather just do it properly once."** It feels like a good decision about their home. Your job is to make that sentence easy to say, not to say it for them.

- **Let their choice reveal the budget.** You've presented every option. Don't tell them what they can afford. Let them show you.
- **Use their words.** Tie Elite back to what they told you: the twenty years they're staying, the 2am call-out they never want again.
- **Make the big one feel reachable.** This is finance's second light touch, tied to the top option: "For the bigger one, some customers just pay it weekly rather than all at once." (Module 6 covers it.)
- **Don't back away from the recommendation.** Letting a family walk away with a dodgy patch, because we were too soft to have the money conversation, is not kindness.

**Selling is service.** If the fix protects their home, recommending it is the job.

> **On the job:** At the Carlingford re-pipe, the keen partner turns to the other: "We've patched it three times this year. I'd rather just do it properly once." Stop talking. That sentence is worth more coming from them than anything you could add.

## Scope. Price. Silence. Top sellers pause about two seconds after the price. The silence feels long to you, not to them

Big numbers usually go wrong in the last ten seconds. Three steps:

1. **Scope.** What they get, in plain English, in their terms.
2. **Price.** Say the number clearly, once, without apologising or softening it.
3. **Silence.** Then stop talking.

Top sellers pause **about two seconds** after stating the price. After an objection, star reps pause **five times longer**, while average reps talk faster.

**The silence feels long to you, not to them.** You're waiting. They're thinking about the number, the options and how it would work. Count to five in your head if you need to.

The flinch starts before the silence, in your voice. **Say a $30K number out loud without flinching.** Practise it in the van, in pairs, in the mirror, until it's boring. A number you say comfortably sounds like the right price. A number you mumble sounds negotiable.

> **Say it like this:** "The full re-pipe is $28,000." [Then stop talking. Let them speak first.]

*Source: Gong Labs call analysis (519,000 recorded sales calls), cited in Proximity's October 2026 "Selling the Big Job" session. It shows what top sellers tend to do (a correlation), not a guaranteed result.*

## Never justify and never discount to fill the quiet. Whoever speaks next should be the customer

The silence after the price is uncomfortable, and discomfort makes good sellers do two expensive things.

**They justify.** "I know it sounds like a lot, but..." followed by reasons nobody asked for. It tells the customer you don't believe the number yourself.

**They discount.** **Most techs discount before the customer has even objected.** Don't. Never drop the price to ease your own discomfort, and even when they do push back:

- **No free discounts.** If the price comes down, scope comes out. Otherwise they learn our first number was fake, and they'll always push.
- **You can't change a price anyway.** Only Harriet or Sadie can. Never offer to "see what the office can do".
- **Pause again.** After an objection, the best sellers get quieter, not faster. Then find out what's behind it (module 7).

**Whoever speaks next should be the customer.** Often they'll break the silence with a question about timing or payment. That's where finance comes home: "Like I mentioned, I can turn that into a weekly figure in about two minutes."

> **On the job:** You say "$28,000" in a Carlingford kitchen and the room goes quiet. Three seconds in, you hear yourself start: "But look, if that's too much..." Stop. Let the quiet do its work, and let them speak first.
