## 'I can't afford it' almost never means they can't pay: they don't see the value yet, they're scared of the number, or nobody gave them a way to say yes

You've heard "I can't afford it" more times than you can count. The risk, years in, is that you hear it as a no. You start packing up the tablet, or worse, you reach for a lower number.

**"I can't afford it" almost never means they can't pay.** It usually means one of three things:

- **They don't see the value yet.** The problem, the consequences or what the fix means for them hasn't landed.
- **They're scared of the number.** A lump sum feels impossible, even when a weekly figure wouldn't.
- **Nobody gave them a way to say yes.** One price is a yes or no. Four options turn the question into "which one?"

Each has its own tool: the value and the cost of doing nothing, payment options in weekly numbers, or a lower option with fewer lines. None of them is a discount.

**Our job is to remove the barrier, not drop the price.**

> **On the job:** A young family in Kellyville, and the sewer's blocked for the third time this year. The camera shows roots through a cracked section. You present all four Drain Fix-for-Good options, Elite first. The dad sighs: "Mate, we just can't afford that right now." Which of the three is it? And what's the very next thing you say?

## Separate can't from won't: 'Totally fair. Can I ask what feels like too much about it?'

Most of the time it's **"I don't want to spend that", not "I can't"**. Stay calm and curious, never apologetic. Ask, then let them talk. **Their answer tells you which tool to reach for next.**

> **Say it like this:** "Totally fair. Can I ask what feels like too much about it?"

What you hear back points to the tool:

- "It's more than we expected in one go." Scared of the number: the weekly figure from HomePro.
- "Do we really need all of that?" Value: back to the findings and the cost of doing nothing, or step down an option.
- "We honestly don't have it right now." Genuinely tight: a lower option with fewer lines, or finance.

That question is the heart of **the objection turnaround**:

1. Pause and acknowledge.
2. Ask one clarifying question.
3. Reframe: value, consequence or a lower tier.
4. Ask for the next step.

**One objection at a time.** Answering three worries in one breath sounds like a pitch.

And if there's no objection yet, only silence after the price, hold it. The silence feels long to you, not to them.

*The turnaround is our version of a principle from Jeb Blount's book Objections. It gets easier when you've qualified pain, budget and decision before presenting, a Sandler principle, so the money conversation is never a surprise.*

## Feel, felt, found: disagree without arguing

**An old line, and it still works.** It lets you disagree with the customer without arguing with them.

> **Say it like this:** "I understand how you feel. Plenty of our customers felt the same. What they found was…"

- **Feel** says you've heard them. No defending, no correcting.
- **Felt** says they're normal. Other sensible people thought the same.
- **Found** gives them new information from other people's experience, not your opinion.

**The "found" has to be true.** Use what you've actually seen: homes that patched it, the footage, the return visits. Never invent a customer story.

Feel, felt, found is one way to do the reframe in the turnaround. Another is a **consequence question**: instead of telling them what happens if they leave it, ask, and let them say it themselves. That idea comes from Jeremy Miner's NEPQ. Use it gently. In a premium, no-pressure business it should sound curious, never like a trap.

> **On the job:** In a Hurstville home, the owner says relining sounds like overkill and he'll just get the drain cleared again. You agree it's a fair thought, then ask, "How many times has it been cleared so far?" He counts three. You tell him plenty of customers felt the same, then show him the footage of the roots coming back through the crack.

## Never discount for free: if the price comes down, scope comes out. 'I can't do that price on the full job. If we do just the urgent part today, here's where we land'

**Price only comes down when scope comes out.** Otherwise they learn our first number was fake, and they'll push on every quote after this one.

> **Say it like this:** "I can't do that price on the full job. If we do just the urgent part today, here's where we land."

The rules don't bend for experience:

- **You can't discount.** Only Harriet (Sales Manager) or Sadie (Office Manager) can change a price.
- **Never say "let me see what the office can do".** It's a discount by another name, and it tells the customer the price is soft.
- **Step down an option instead.** Cheaper means fewer lines, never a lower price on the same lines.

On a $1,000 job, the profit is about $200. Take 10% off and you've given away $100, half the profit, for the same work. Commission is 5% of sales paid in full, so it comes off your pay too.

> **On the job:** On a Premium Whole-Home Water Protection quote in Concord, the owner asks, "Can you do it cheaper?" You use the line, then show Standard: the leak fixed, the flexi hoses in the affected area replaced and a pressure test, with the rest of the house left for later. Every line is the same price it was. They've just chosen fewer of them.

## 'You're 30% dearer': compare scope line by line against price-led competitors

Price-led competitors compete on price and speed. **We compete on trust and quality.** So when a customer says "you're 30% dearer", don't argue about the number and never badmouth the other company. **Compare the scope, line by line.**

A cheaper quote almost always has a smaller scope. Put them side by side and check:

- **The price**: is theirs fixed, or an hourly rate that can grow?
- **The fix**: the root cause, or just the symptom?
- **The related items**: are the valves, hoses or drain you found included?
- **Exclusions**: what isn't covered, and who pays when it turns up?
- **After the job**: our lifetime labour warranty and same-day return. [CONFIRM: approved wording for the warranty and same-day return, including what's excluded]

Let the gaps speak for themselves. The customer will see what's missing. If they still want a smaller job, step down an option. **Never match a price.**

> **Say it like this:** "That's fair to check. Can we put them side by side? I want to make sure you're comparing the same job."

> **On the job:** In Epping, a customer shows you a hot water quote that's 30% cheaper than your Premium. Side by side, it's the unit only: no tempering valve, no isolation valve, no disposal of the old unit. Your Essential is the closer match, and now she can see it.

## 'We need to think about it' / 'talk to my partner': agree the follow-up time and what they'll decide

"We need to think about it" usually means something is unresolved: a question they haven't asked, a worry about the number, or a person who isn't in the room. Push against it and it becomes a firm no.

Acknowledge it warmly, then ask which part they're unsure of. Often it's one thing you can deal with now. If they genuinely need time, **agree two things before you leave**:

1. **When you'll follow up**: a day and a time, not "I'll call you sometime".
2. **What they'll decide by then**: which option, or that it's not for now.

If the partner wasn't there, offer to go through it with both of them. That's why discovery asks who decides.

**A respectful "not yet" with a booked follow-up is a win, not a loss.** Your up-front agreement already allowed for it. No fake deadlines, no "prices go up next week". Put the follow-up in ServiceTitan as the next action and date.

> **Say it like this:** "Of course, it's a big decision. Can we lock in a time, say Thursday evening? By then you'll know whether it's Premium, Standard or not for now."

> **On the job:** In Maroubra, she says, "Money's tight right now. We'll have to think about it." You separate can't from won't first: "Totally fair. Can I ask what feels like too much about it?" It's the lump sum, so you offer the weekly figure. They still want to talk, so you book the call before you leave.

## 'Can you sharpen your pencil?' from builders and property managers

Builders and property managers ask this a lot. It's a reflex, part of how they buy, and they'll ask whether the price is fair or not.

**The answer is the same as at any kitchen table.** You can't discount, and only Harriet or Sadie can change a price. Never say "let me see what the office can do". Step down an option instead. A trade customer who learns your price moves once will haggle on every quote after that.

What you can sharpen is **the scope**:

- **Builders**: be exact about what's in and out, who does the making good, and what depends on what's found once things are opened up.
- **Property managers**: work to their approval limit, and never go over it without written approval. If the proper fix costs more, send options with photos so the landlord can choose.

Larger strata work goes to Bella (Business Partnerships) and commercial work to Alex (Commercial Manager), so bring them in early.

> **Say it like this:** "I can't sharpen the price, but I can sharpen the scope. Tell me what you need us to cover, and I'll show you where that lands."

> **On the job:** A builder renovating in Five Dock wants your rough-in and fit-off quote "sharper". You go back through the scope together, and he decides to leave the outdoor taps for a later stage. Those lines come out. The price came down because the scope did, not because you blinked.

## Selling is service: letting a family walk away with a dodgy patch because we were too soft to have the money conversation is not kindness

Backing off the money conversation feels kind in the moment. It isn't. **Selling is service. If the fix protects their home, recommending it is the job.**

The leak doesn't pause while they save up:

- **Today**: a planned fix at today's price.
- **In three months**: water damage, and a bigger bill.
- **The worst night**: an emergency call-out at after-hours rates.

It works both ways. If something can genuinely wait, say so. Never inflate a finding and never invent urgency. Consequences, not scare tactics: tell them what you'd tell your own family, then let them choose.

**Deal review is where this gets real.** Every week you bring one won and one lost quote. Your sign-off for this module: workshop a real lost quote in deal review, then go back to that customer.

> **Say it like this:** "Honestly, what happens if we leave it is the part I'd worry about."

> **On the job:** Last month you lost a reline in Sutherland. The owner said money was tight, and you said, "No worries, give us a call if it gets worse." In deal review, the group spots it: you never asked what felt like too much, and finance never came up. You call her back, book a time, and this time you have the money conversation.
