## The diagnostic flow: listen → inspect → test → root cause → explain

Every service call follows the same five steps:

1. **Listen.** Let the customer explain what's been happening, in their own words. Ask how long, how often and what's changed. Technicians who talk too much miss the details that matter.
2. **Inspect.** Look at the whole area, not just the part they pointed at.
3. **Test.** Prove what's going on rather than guessing.
4. **Root cause.** Find out *why* it happened, not just *what* happened. Fixing a symptom means a return trip later.
5. **Explain.** Tell the customer what you found and what it means for them, in plain English.

Then **fix what they called about, and check the rest of the system.** The call is often a symptom of a bigger need.

> **On the job:** A customer in Epping says the toilet keeps running. The cistern valve is the obvious fix. Testing shows the mains pressure is high and wearing out valves around the house. Fixing the valve solves today. Finding the pressure problem solves the next three call-outs.

## The whole-home health check: pressure/PRV, flexi hoses, isolation valves, hot water age, stop tap, meter test, gully, visible leaks

On every visit, after you've fixed what they called about, run the **whole-home health check**:

- **Pressure and the PRV**: is the mains pressure where it should be?
- **Flexi hoses**: any perished, kinked or corroded hoses under sinks and toilets?
- **Isolation valves**: are they there, and do they work?
- **Hot water age**: how old is the unit? Units around 8 years or older get flagged for follow-up.
- **The stop tap**: can the customer find it and turn it off in an emergency?
- **A meter test**: any sign of a hidden leak?
- **The gully**: is the overflow relief gully clear?
- **Visible leaks**: anything dripping, stained or damp?

**Record the "all good" items too.** The health check feeds the customer's report and future follow-up, so a clean result is still valuable. And **never inflate a finding.** Calm, honest advice is what earns the reviews.

> **Say it like this:** "While I'm here, I always do a quick check of the rest of the plumbing, so you know where things stand. Most of it's usually fine."

## Show, don't tell: photos and camera footage

Customers can't see inside their walls, drains or hot water unit. **Showing beats telling.**

- Photograph what you find: the corroded hose, the rust at the base of the unit, the stained ceiling.
- Use camera footage for drains, so the customer sees the roots or the crack for themselves.
- Number your photos and refer to them in your notes, for example "photo 3: corroded flexi hose under the kitchen sink".
- Save everything to the job in ServiceTitan.

When a customer sees the problem with their own eyes, you don't have to convince them. They understand it, they trust you more, and they make their own decision.

> **On the job:** In a Hornsby home with a slow shower drain, you show the owner the camera footage on your tablet: a root mass two metres in. She stops asking "is this really necessary?" and starts asking "what are my options?"

## Explaining problems in plain English

Explain what you found the way you'd explain it to a mate, not to another plumber.

- **Lead with what it means for them**, not the technical name of the part.
- **Use a simple comparison** where it helps. Don't force one.
- **Describe the consequence honestly**: what's likely to happen if they leave it. That's information, not a scare tactic.
- **Keep it calm.** Never use words like "illegal" or "dangerous" to push a decision. Say it's "worth having a licensed look at" or "worth addressing before it becomes a bigger issue".

Then stop and let them ask questions. A customer who understands the problem is ready to hear the options.

> **Say it like this:** "This hose is the one that feeds your kitchen tap. The outside's starting to rust and fray. If it lets go, it'll leak under the sink until someone turns the water off. It's a quick job to swap it out while I'm here."
