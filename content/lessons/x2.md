## Who does what: you sell. The Install Manager (Charlotte, interim) owns delivery, the customer's happiness and the balance payment. (Service Techs captain the planned jobs they sell, but Sales Techs don't.)

Your job is to **sell**. Not to run the install, chase the balance or settle the customer's nerves the week before. Every hour you spend managing a job you've already sold is an hour you're not in front of a lead.

Who owns what:

- **You:** the sale. Discovery, options, the signed approval, the 50% deposit at signing and a complete handover.
- **Kim (residential dispatch):** books the job and accepts the handover with the Install Manager.
- **The Install Manager:** delivery, the customer's happiness and collecting the balance on completion. The role is being recruited, so **Charlotte (Operations Manager) is covering it**.
- **Harriet (Sales Manager):** reviews your handovers.

**After a complete handover, the job belongs to the Install Manager.** Not to you until it's paid, and not to dispatch.

Service Techs captain the planned jobs they sell. That's their role, not yours. Sales Techs hand over and get back on the road.

> **On the job:** Three weeks after you sold a re-pipe in Cherrybrook, the customer rings your mobile: when's the installer coming, and can the balance be paid in two parts? Who should be answering these questions, and what does the call tell you about your handover?

## Why the handover is the last part of the sale: an incomplete handover is the only thing that drags you off the road and away from your next lead

Most sellers think the sale ends at the signature. It doesn't. **The handover is the last part of the sale.**

Think about what pulls you away from your next lead. It's almost never the customer you've just signed. It's the installer who can't find the scope, the Install Manager asking what the customer was promised, or a scope change halfway through a job you under-scoped. **An incomplete handover is the only thing that drags you off the road.**

It costs you money too. **Commission is paid on sales paid in full**, so a job that stalls, gets re-scoped or comes back hurts your pay as well as the installer's.

The test is simple: **could the installer do this job without ever calling you?** If not, the handover isn't finished.

> **On the job:** You're mid-presentation in Wahroonga when an installer calls. The bathroom you sold last week has no tapware selection in the handover, and the customer wants a different mixer. You lose ten minutes and your thread with the customer in front of you, and the installer loses the morning.

## The handover pack: scope (in and out), materials and specials, photos and video, access, estimated hours, customer notes and expectations

Every sold job goes over in ServiceTitan with the same pack:

- **Scope, in and out.** Each item of work in plain English, line by line from the pricebook, plus what's *not* included.
- **Materials and specials.** Anything that isn't van stock: the unit model, tapware selections, anything with a lead time.
- **Photos and video.** Numbered, saved to the job and referred to in the scope.
- **Access.** Parking, keys, pets, strata rules, a tenant, a tight subfloor.
- **Estimated hours.** Realistic combined hours, for how the installer will actually pace the job.
- **Customer notes and expectations.** What they care about, what they've been told and what will make them nervous on the day.

**Photos support the scope. They never replace it.** A scope that says "see photos" isn't a scope: the installer can't tell from a photo what you sold or what the customer expects.

The installer reads your handover the day before. If they'd need to call you to understand it, it isn't finished.

> **On the job:** It's 6:40pm, your last lead of a long day, and the customer has just signed a $16K Elite bathroom in Castle Hill. You're tempted to type "see photos" and go home. Take the ten minutes: scope in and out, the tapware selection, where to park, and the fact that she works from home and needs a working toilet every day.

## Signed approval and the 50% deposit collected by you at signing, before you leave, then booked with Kim. No deposit, no booking and no materials ordered

Before you leave the driveway, two things are done:

1. **The approval is signed** in ServiceTitan, for the option the customer chose.
2. **The 50% deposit is collected by you, at signing.** Not by the office next week, and not by the installer on the day.

Then the job is booked with Kim.

**No deposit, no booking and no materials ordered.** The deposit is what turns a signed approval into a scheduled job. Without it, the job sits in limbo while the customer assumes it's happening.

Signing is also the easiest moment to collect it. The customer has just decided and the scope is fresh. Asking a week later turns it into a chase.

## Setting the customer up: who'll contact them next (the Install Manager), what happens on install day, and that the balance is due on completion

Before you leave the kitchen table, tell the customer three things:

1. **Who'll contact them next: the Install Manager**, to book everything in. Not you. "Call me any time" feels generous, but it puts you back in the middle of a job you've handed over.
2. **What happens on install day.** The installer calls when they're on the way, protects the home with shoe covers and drop sheets, and walks them through the scope and signed quote before starting.
3. **The balance is due on completion**, collected by the Install Manager.

Keep it to that. No promises about dates you don't control, and no extras that aren't in the scope. Anything you promise at the table that isn't in the handover becomes the installer's problem on the day.

Then **write it into the handover**: what you told them, and anything they're anxious about.

> **Say it like this:** "From here, our Install Manager will call you next to book everything in. On the day, the installer will ring when they're on the way and run through the scope with you before they start. The balance is due when the job's finished."

## Scope accuracy: your hours set the installer's pay, and under-scoping causes the scope-change calls and warranty returns that land back on you

**Your hours set the installer's pay.** Installers are paid on efficiency: the gap between the hours estimated for the job and the hours it actually takes. [CONFIRM: exact install pay plan]

When you under-scope, the installer pays first. They run over, lose efficiency pay and finish late. Then it comes back to you:

- **Scope-change calls.** The installer finds work that isn't in the scope. They have to stop, photograph it, explain it, re-price it from the pricebook and get signed approval before carrying on. The customer hears a new number, and someone rings you to ask why.
- **Warranty returns.** A job squeezed into too few hours is a rushed job, and rushed jobs come back. Under the lifetime labour warranty, every return costs the business.

Both cost profit, and both hold up the job being paid in full.

So scope it honestly: realistic combined hours, the access, the age of the pipework, anything that will slow the job down. When hours run over on work that's in the scope, the customer doesn't pay for them. We do.

> **On the job:** You sell a re-pipe in an older Beecroft house on standard hours, without mentioning the low, tight subfloor. The installer runs a full day over, the scope-change call comes back to you, and the customer starts wondering what else you missed.
