## Before you arrive: confirm the day before, call when you're on the way

A great visit starts before you knock. **Confirm the day before, and call when you're on the way.**

The day before, open tomorrow's jobs in ServiceTitan and check the address, the arrival window, what each job is for, the access notes (parking, keys, pets, a tenant or a strata building) and that you've got the parts you'll need. If anything is unclear, sort it out now with dispatch (Kim for residential) or, on installs, the Install Manager (Charlotte, interim). **On the day, call the customer when you're on the way**, before you pull out or hands-free. It tells them when you'll arrive, lets you check access and parking, and gives them time to get ready.

**If you're running late, call ahead.** Ring the customer before their arrival window passes, and let dispatch know so they can adjust the rest of the day. A call turns a complaint into a courtesy. Turning up late without a word is how a good job ends in a bad review.

> **On the job:** Your first job runs over, and you're going to be well past the arrival window for a customer in Gordon who has taken the morning off work. You could push on and hope, ask dispatch to ring her, or call her yourself. What do you do, and what do you say?

## Arrival: on time, in uniform, shoe covers on, drop sheets down

**The first 30 seconds set the tone.** Before you've touched anything, the customer has decided whether they trust you. And they judge the whole company by you.

The standard on every job, big or small:

- **On time**, or you've called ahead.
- **Park considerately.** Don't block their driveway or the neighbours'.
- **In uniform**, clean and recognisable.
- **Introduce yourself clearly**: your name, Proximity, and why you're there.
- **Shoe covers on** before you step inside.
- **Drop sheets down** from the door to the work area, before your tools come in.

A tech who protects the home before touching anything earns trust straight away. A tech who walks dirt down the hallway has lost it before the job starts.

**Never blame dispatch, the office or another tech.** If the customer is annoyed about an earlier visit, like a tech who was late, own it for the company: "I'm sorry about that. Let's get this sorted for you today."

> **Say it like this:** "Hi, I'm [your name] from Proximity Plumbing. I'm here to sort out your hot water today. I'll just pop these shoe covers on before I come in."

> **On the job:** An older customer in Wahroonga opens the door only partway. You step back, introduce yourself, tell her what you're there for and pull your shoe covers on at the doorstep. She opens the door properly. You've just made the rest of the visit easier for both of you.

## Tell them what's going to happen: what you'll check, roughly how long, and that you'll explain options before doing any work

People relax when they know the plan. **Before you start, tell the customer what's going to happen.** Anxious customers hover, worry about the bill and question everything.

Cover three things, in plain English:

1. **What you'll check**: what they called about, plus a quick look at the rest of the plumbing.
2. **Roughly how long it will take**, and anything that affects them, like the water being off.
3. **That you'll explain before you do any work.** You'll show them what you found and give them their options with a fixed price, and they decide.

The third point matters most. It tells the customer nothing happens without their OK, which is our no-hidden-costs promise in action. It also means the four options feel like part of the plan, not a sales pitch.

On installs, this is where you walk them through the signed scope: what's included, what isn't, and when the water or gas will be off.

> **Say it like this:** "Here's how I'll go about it. I'll look at the hot water first, then do a quick check of the rest of the plumbing. That usually takes about [time]. Before I do any work, I'll show you what I've found and give you your options with a fixed price, and you can decide. Sound OK?"

## Listen first: confirm what they called about in their own words

**Before you diagnose anything, let the customer tell you what's happening, in their own words.** Then say it back to check you've got it right.

Ask, then listen:

- What's been happening?
- How long has it been going on, and how often?
- Has anything changed, or has anyone had a go at fixing it?
- What's worrying you most about it?

Don't interrupt, and don't diagnose at the front door. The detail you need is often in the second or third thing they say.

Saying it back proves you listened, catches a misunderstanding before you chase the wrong problem, and tells you what matters to them. Listen for lines like "we're selling next year", "Mum lives with us" or "I never want another emergency", and note them in the job. They shape the options you build later.

> **Say it like this:** "So the hot water runs out halfway through the second shower, it started a few weeks ago, and with five of you in the house it's a real problem. Have I got that right?"

> **On the job:** A customer in Engadine says the toilet "makes a noise". You let her explain, and it turns out it refills on its own through the night and wakes the baby. You repeat it back, and she says, "Yes, exactly." Now you know the real problem, and what fixing it means to her.

## Explain before you act; no surprises on time or price

**Nothing happens until the customer understands it and agrees to it.** That's what a fixed upfront price and no hidden costs mean on the ground.

- **Explain what you found**, in plain English, with photos.
- **Give the price before you start**, from the pricebook.
- **Give a realistic time**, and if it changes, tell them as soon as you know.

If something changes once you're into the job, **stop**. Photograph it, show the customer, explain the options and the new price from the pricebook, and get their signed approval in ServiceTitan before you carry on. Unapproved extra work breaks the promise, however good your intentions. On installs, if the site doesn't match the scope, call the Install Manager (Charlotte, interim) before you continue.

Be honest about time too. "About two hours" that takes 90 minutes looks good. "Half an hour" that takes two hours costs you their trust.

> **Say it like this:** "I've found something I didn't expect. Before I do anything else, let me show you, and we'll go through your options. Nothing changes without your OK."

> **On the job:** Halfway through replacing a shower mixer in Maroubra, you find the pipe behind it is badly corroded. You stop, photograph it, show the owner and explain what it means. She chooses her option and signs for it before you pick the tools back up. No surprises on the invoice.

## Leave it cleaner than you found it and take the rubbish away

**Clean-up is part of the job, not an optional extra.** A perfect job with a mess left behind gets remembered as a mess.

Before you pack up:

- **Take all the rubbish away**: old parts, packaging, offcuts and the old unit. Don't leave it in their bins.
- **Wipe down** the fixtures and surfaces you worked on, and check for marks, dust and water.
- **Put things back** where they were: furniture, the things from under the sink, the bins.
- **Lift the drop sheets last**, carefully, so the dust leaves with them.
- **Check your path** from the work area to the door, and outside where you parked.
- **Do a final check for leaks** before you call it done.

Then walk the customer through the finished work. A clean site makes that walk-through easy. It's also what they'll remember when Chloe makes her happy call, and when they're deciding whether to leave a review.

> **On the job:** You've finished a laundry tap swap in Cronulla. The work is perfect, but there's a cardboard box on the bench, copper offcuts in the cupboard and a wet footprint on the hallway tiles. Two more minutes turns "good job" into "they left it spotless".

## Tenants, property managers and strata buildings: access, keys, approvals

The person who opens the door isn't always the person who decides. **Know who approves the work, who gives access and who pays, before you start.**

**Tenant jobs: the property manager approves, and the tenant gives access.** Tenants deserve the same care as any owner, but they're not the decision-maker. Extra work they ask for goes to the property manager. Never exceed the property manager's approval limit without written approval.

**Strata buildings: record whether the problem is on common property or in a lot.** It changes who approves and who pays, so photograph where the problem is and what it affects. Larger strata work goes to Bella (Business Partnerships), and commercial work goes to Alex (Commercial Manager).

**Access and keys:**

- Check the access notes before you go: who's meeting you, where the keys are, building access and parking.
- Treat keys and access codes like cash. Never leave a property unlocked or a key where it shouldn't be.
- Note in the job how you got in and where the keys went.
- In a strata building, follow the building's rules for lifts, parking and shutting off shared services.

> **On the job:** The hot water has failed at a rental in Parramatta. The tenant asks you to replace the dripping kitchen mixer while you're there: "The landlord won't mind." It might be a good idea, but it isn't the tenant's call. You photograph it, note it and raise it for the property manager to approve.

## If a customer complains on site: stay calm, don't argue or blame, no refund or discount promises, call the Install Manager (Charlotte, interim) or the office, log it

Even on a good job, things go wrong. **How you handle it on the spot decides whether it becomes a bad review or a problem solved.**

1. **Stay calm.** Don't match their energy.
2. **Listen to all of it** without interrupting. Acknowledge how they feel, and ask what's worrying them most.
3. **Don't argue or blame.** Not dispatch, not the office, not another tech, not the customer.
4. **Don't promise a refund or discount.** Not "I'll knock something off", not "I'll see what the office can do", and never in exchange for a review.
5. **Call the Install Manager (Charlotte, interim) or the office** while you're still on site.
6. **Log it**, so the Customer Service team (Abigail, Elle and Chloe) takes over the complaint. [CONFIRM: how the Customer Service team logs and resolves complaints]

If the job is just taking longer, you can calmly remind them the price is fixed, so the extra time doesn't change what they pay. Never use it to shut them down.

> **Say it like this:** "I understand, that's frustrating. I can't promise a refund or discount, but I'm calling the office now to log it, and our Customer Service team will take it from there."

> **On the job:** A hot water install in Cherrybrook is running well over. The owner says, "This was meant to take two hours. It's been four. I want money off." You listen, acknowledge it, explain what's left, and call Charlotte to log it before you carry on.
