## Common tap, mixer and cistern repairs

Taps, mixers and cisterns are the bread and butter of service work. **First-time fix means the fix that lasts, not the fastest one.**

The common repairs:

- **Jumper-valve taps**: a worn washer, jumper valve or O-ring. If the seat is pitted, a new washer won't seal for long, so reseat it or offer a new tap.
- **Ceramic disc taps**: a worn or cracked cartridge. Match the cartridge to the tap.
- **Mixers**: cartridges are often brand-specific. Photograph the brand and model before you strip it.
- **Cisterns**: a worn inlet valve (keeps filling or runs down the overflow) or a worn flush valve seal (water trickling into the pan).

Whatever you fix, **replace like-for-like, or offer an upgrade from the pricebook.** Never fit whatever happens to be in the van, and never guess a price.

While you're there, check the isolation valve and the flexi hose feeding the fixture. A new washer sitting on a perished flexi is the next call-out waiting to happen.

> **On the job:** A basin tap in a Gladesville bathroom is dripping. You could swap the washer in a few minutes and leave. But the seat is pitted, the tap is old and the flexi underneath is rusting. A washer on its own will have you back. Fix it properly, show the customer the flexi, and give them the options.

## Toilet leaks, suites and pans

Toilet leaks are easy to miss and expensive to ignore. A cistern running quietly into the pan wastes water day and night, and the customer often only notices when the bill arrives.

Find out where the water is going:

1. **Into the pan.** Put a few drops of food dye in the cistern and wait without flushing. Colour in the pan means the flush valve seal is leaking.
2. **Down the overflow.** The inlet valve isn't shutting off, or the water level is set too high.
3. **Onto the floor.** Check the cistern-to-pan seal, the flexi and stop valve, and the pan connector. Water around the base after a flush can mean the pan connector, so wear the right PPE for sewage.

Sometimes repair isn't the right answer: a cracked pan or cistern, parts that are no longer made, or an old suite the customer is tired of. Then a new suite goes into the options, from the pricebook. **Measure the set-out and note the pan type (S-trap or P-trap) and the cistern type**, so the right suite is ordered first time.

> **On the job:** A customer in Concord says the water bill has jumped, but the toilet "looks fine". The dye test puts colour in the pan: a silent leak through the flush valve. You show her, fix it, and point out that the cistern's isolation valve is seized too.

## Pressure, PRVs and water hammer

High mains pressure is behind a lot of everyday call-outs. It wears out washers, cartridges and cistern valves faster, strains flexi hoses, and can make the hot water relief valve discharge. **If the same kind of part keeps failing in a house, test the pressure.**

Test it with a gauge and compare it with the allowed limit. [VERIFY: the maximum allowable water pressure under AS/NZS 3500 and Sydney Water's guidance] If it's high and there's no PRV, or the PRV has failed, that's the root cause, and it belongs in your options.

**Water hammer** is the bang or shudder in the pipes when a tap or appliance valve shuts off quickly, often from a washing machine, dishwasher or quarter-turn tap. The usual causes are high pressure, quick-closing valves and pipework that isn't clipped properly.

The fixes match the causes: a PRV to bring the pressure down, water hammer arrestors where appliances shut off fast, and clips for loose pipes. They're all in the Whole-Home Water Protection bundle: a PRV goes into Premium when mains pressure is high, and water hammer arrestors are in Elite.

> **On the job:** In a Ryde house, the customer says this is the third cistern valve in two years. Your gauge shows high pressure and a failed PRV. The new valve fixes today. The PRV fixes the pattern.

> **Say it like this:** "The pressure coming into your house is higher than it should be. That's likely why your valves keep wearing out. Bringing it down protects everything after it."

## No part on board: avoiding a return trip

Sometimes you open it up and the part you need isn't in the van. **The aim is to find an option that avoids a return trip**, because a return trip doubles the cost of the job. The customer still pays the same fixed price, so the second visit comes straight out of the profit, and that's only about 20% to start with.

Work through it in this order:

1. **Check the van properly.** Is there a like-for-like part, or a compatible one from the pricebook that does the job just as well?
2. **Offer an upgrade you do have.** If the cartridge for a tired old mixer isn't on board, a new mixer from the pricebook may be the better option anyway. Present it honestly alongside the others and let the customer choose.
3. **Check whether a nearby supplier has it.** If a quick pickup works, call Kim (residential dispatch) before you go, so the rest of the day can be adjusted.
4. **If a return really can't be avoided**, make it safe, explain why honestly, order the part and book the return properly. Never leave a bodge behind.

Then fix the cause. If it was a part you should have had, it goes on tonight's restock.

> **Say it like this:** "I don't carry the cartridge for this brand of mixer. I can order it in and come back, or I can replace the mixer today. Here's what each option looks like."
