## Understand the problem: inspect, ask questions, take photos and video

The rule for every estimate is simple: **diagnose first, price last.** A price you give before you understand the problem is a guess, and guesses either lose the job or lose the profit.

Start by listening. Ask what's been happening, how long it's been going on, and how it's affecting them day to day. Then inspect the whole area, not just the part they pointed at.

Take photos and video as you go. They're not just for the job record:

- They show the customer what you found, because **showing beats telling**.
- They protect you if anything is questioned later.
- They let the office and the installer see the job before they arrive.

Record what's fine as well as what's wrong. "Isolation valves all good" is useful information too.

> **On the job:** A customer in Ermington says the kitchen tap drips. Before you touch it, you ask how long it's been dripping, check under the sink and photograph a corroded flexi hose behind the bin. The tap was the reason for the call. The hose is the bigger risk.

## Define the scope: what's in and what's not

The scope is the written description of exactly what we'll do. It's the foundation of the fixed-price, no-hidden-costs promise, so it has to be clear.

Every estimate says **what's included and what isn't**. If it's vague, the customer fills the gaps with their own assumptions, and those assumptions turn into arguments on install day.

Good scope wording:

- names each item of work in plain English
- says what's **not** included (for example, making good the plaster or tiling after access)
- notes anything that depends on what's found once things are opened up

The installer will read your scope the day before the job. If they'd have to call you to understand it, it isn't finished.

> **Say it like this:** "This price covers replacing the hot water unit, the new valves and taking the old unit away. It doesn't include patching the wall where the old pipes come out. I've written that down so we're both clear."

## Estimate time and materials realistically

Customers pay a fixed price, so the hours and materials behind it have to be right. **Realistic hours matter because the installer's time and the business's profit both depend on them.**

When hours are under-estimated:

- the installer runs over and loses efficiency pay
- every extra hour comes straight out of the profit
- the job runs late and the customer notices

When materials are missed, someone makes a supply run mid-job, which costs more time and more profit.

Use the pricebook and templates for the hours, and think about the actual site: access, how old the pipework is, stairs, roof spaces, and anything that will slow the job down. If something will add time, say so in the scope rather than hoping it won't.

> **On the job:** A re-pipe in an older Lane Cove house has a low, tight subfloor. The template hours assume easy access. You note the access problem in the scope so the hours are right before anyone picks up a tool.

## Build the four options (Essential, Standard, Premium, Elite) on every quote

Every quote gets **four options**, and **Elite is presented first**:

- **Essential**: bare bones. It fixes today's problem.
- **Standard**: a solid, sensible choice.
- **Premium**: fixes the root cause. Most homes land here.
- **Elite**: the best outcome, future-proofed.

**Never quote one job when the site shows more.** Standard, Premium and Elite bundle the related items you found. Use the five standard bundles where they fit: Hot Water, Whole-Home Water Protection, Bathroom Refresh, Drain Fix-for-Good and Landlord Compliance Pack.

Every line comes from the pricebook. There are no bundle discounts. If the customer wants it cheaper, they choose an option with fewer lines.

> **On the job:** At that Ermington kitchen, Essential fixes the drip. Standard adds the corroded flexi hose. Premium replaces all the flexi hoses under the sinks. Elite adds isolation valves and a Care Plan. The customer picks; you don't decide for them.

## The pre-send check: price, wording, inclusions, exclusions, warranty

Before any estimate goes to a customer, check five things:

1. **Price**: every line comes from the pricebook or an approved template, with no hand-typed prices.
2. **Wording**: plain English, with no jargon the customer won't understand.
3. **Inclusions**: everything we'll do is listed.
4. **Exclusions**: everything we won't do is stated.
5. **Warranty**: it's described in the approved wording. [CONFIRM: approved warranty wording]

It takes a minute, and it saves the arguments, the re-quotes and the lost profit that come from a sloppy estimate.

If you've built the same estimate more than once, it should probably be a template. That's the next module.

> **Say it like this:** "Before I send this through, let me just check it covers everything we talked about and nothing we didn't."
