## The rule: no completion without payment and customer sign-off in ServiceTitan

**A job isn't finished until it's paid and signed off in ServiceTitan, before you leave.** No exceptions without office approval.

Leaving with the work done but the money not collected isn't a finished job. It's an unfinished one that someone in the office now has to chase. And money that isn't collected on site often never gets collected at all.

You're the best person to collect it, because you're standing there at the moment the customer is happiest with the work.

Finish every job in the same order:

1. **Clean up**, and leave it cleaner than you found it.
2. **Walk the customer through the work**, with the before-and-after photos.
3. **Explain the warranty** in the approved wording.
4. **Take payment and get sign-off** in ServiceTitan.
5. **Ask happy customers for a review.**

Before you pack up, **check the payment actually went through** and shows as paid against the right job. A payment that was attempted but failed is the same as no payment.

Paid and signed off is also what makes the work actually count.

## 'I already paid', 'I'm not home' and 'invoice me later'

Three things you'll hear, and what to do with each.

**"I already paid."** Sometimes it's true, sometimes it's a mix-up, like paying the deposit and thinking that covered everything. **Check, never assume.** Open the job in ServiceTitan, see what's actually been paid, then state the balance clearly and collect it.

**"I'm not home."** Some jobs are finished with nobody there, like tenant jobs or when access was arranged. Know the payment arrangement before you start. If nothing's arranged, **call the office before you finish** so they can sort it, and note how it's being paid.

**"Just invoice me, I'll pay next week."** Stay friendly and restate the policy: payment is due on completion. Offer the payment options. If they genuinely can't pay today, **call the office now, before you leave**, rather than leaving the job unpaid.

Never threaten to take parts back, never pay for it yourself, and never offer a deal to get it paid. Escalating to the office is the professional move, not a failure.

> **Say it like this:** "Glad you're happy with it! Our policy is payment on completion, so I can take it now. What's easiest for you?"

> **On the job:** A homeowner in Beecroft loves the new kitchen mixer but says payday's Friday. You stay friendly: "No problem. Let me call the office now so we sort it properly before I go." The office decides what happens next. You don't drive off and hope.

## Refusals and escalation: the office logs it, Chloe chases unpaid invoices, and Finance (Carla) takes over from there

Now and then a customer refuses to pay. It's rare, and handled well it becomes an office matter, not a fight on the doorstep.

1. **Stay calm.** Don't argue, and never threaten to undo the work or take parts back.
2. **Find out why.** A refusal is often really a complaint about the work, the time or the price. Listen first. Don't promise a refund or discount.
3. **Document it.** Photos and video of the finished work, and a note of what was said.
4. **Escalate straight away**, while you're still on site. On installs, call the Install Manager (Charlotte, interim). Otherwise, call the office.

From there it's out of your hands. **The office logs it, Chloe (Customer Service) chases unpaid invoices, and Finance (Carla) takes over from there.** Your job is to give them the full picture and the evidence, then follow their direction.

Don't freelance. Don't negotiate the price down to get something paid: techs can't change a price. Your photos, notes and the price the customer approved before you started are what let the office resolve it.

> **On the job:** In Liverpool, a customer says the job "shouldn't have cost that much" and won't pay. You don't argue or drop the price. You calmly remind them of the fixed price they approved before you started, photograph the finished work, and call the office from the driveway.
