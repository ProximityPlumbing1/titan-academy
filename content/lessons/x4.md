## The up-front agreement on arrival: what you'll do, how long, and that at the end you'll show options and they can say yes, no or not yet

Before you inspect anything, agree with the customer **how this visit will run**:

- **What you'll do:** look at the problem they called about, then check the rest of the system.
- **How long it will take**, roughly.
- **What happens at the end:** you'll show them options, and **they can say yes, no or not yet**.

It sounds small, but it changes the whole visit. The customer knows there's no ambush coming, so they stop guarding and start talking. And you've both agreed out loud that "not yet" is a normal outcome, which **takes the pressure off both sides**. You don't have to force a yes, and they don't have to defend against one.

It also earns you the time for proper discovery, because they've agreed to it.

> **On the job:** In Turramurra, the owner meets you at the door: "Just tell me what it'll cost. I've got two other quotes coming." You haven't even seen the unit yet. What do you say in the next thirty seconds?

> **Say it like this:** "Here's how I'll run it. I'll look at the hot water, then check the rest of the plumbing, which takes about [time]. Then I'll show you a few options. You can say yes, no or not yet, and any of those is fine."

## Read the trust signals: forward questions ('how long will it last?', 'when can you start?'), open body language, and new problems they start telling you about

Polite isn't sold. A customer can nod, agree and thank you, and still be guarding every word. Before you present a big job, look for **the trust shift**:

- **Forward questions.** They stop asking defensive ones ("Do we really need all that?") and start asking **"How long will it last?"** and **"When can you start?"** They're picturing the job done.
- **Open body language.** They relax, lean in and stop guarding. The conversation feels easier.
- **New problems.** They start telling you about other things around the house: the toilet that runs, the shower that goes cold. That's trust. They're handing you the rest of the job.

It matters because people don't buy big jobs from someone they don't trust. In one large survey, **81% of consumers said they must trust a brand to do what is right before they'll buy from it.**

> **On the job:** Halfway through the inspection in Epping, the owner says, "While you're here, the laundry tap's been dripping for months." A few minutes later: "If we did the whole lot, how long would it last?" That's the shift, and the new problem belongs in the options.

*Source: Edelman Trust Barometer Special Report, 2019 (8 countries, 16,000 people), cited in Proximity's October 2026 "Selling the Big Job" session. It's survey data about what people say, not a guarantee about any one customer.*

## Present after the shift, not before. Presenting too early is how big jobs go cold

Big jobs rarely go cold because of the price. They go cold because the price came **too early**.

Push a number before the trust shift and you're negotiating against a wall. The customer is still guarding, so every number sounds like a threat and every option sounds like an upsell. Wait for the shift, and the same number lands soft.

So when a guarded customer asks "how much?" in the first ten minutes, acknowledge it and keep going with discovery.

> **Say it like this:** "I'll get to that. Can I ask how long you're planning to stay in the house? It changes what I'd recommend."

What if the shift never comes? That's what the up-front agreement is for: "not yet" is a fair outcome, and a low-trust customer is often won with one great job first (module 3). Just don't mistake your own nerves about the number for their low trust.

> **On the job:** In Gladesville, a polite customer agrees with everything but only asks "Is that really necessary?" You're tempted to present now and get it done. Hold off. Ask how the problem affects them and what they'd like, and present when their questions turn forward.

## Discovery for big decisions: how long they'll stay, what's annoyed them, what they want the house to be like, who decides

A big decision needs more than a diagnosis of the pipes. Before you build the options, find out four things:

- **How long they'll stay.** Selling next year and staying twenty years need very different options.
- **What's annoyed them.** The problems they've lived with, and how often.
- **What they want the house to be like.** No more cold showers, a bathroom they're proud of, never thinking about the plumbing again.
- **Who decides.** Is anyone else part of this decision? If they're not home, how would the customer like to bring them in?

Ask about their **goals as well as their problems**. A customer who tells you what they're hoping for has told you which option fits.

Then **ask, and listen**. Technicians who talk too much miss the details that justify the bigger option. Stay curious, ask one question at a time, and let the customer do most of the talking.

> **Say it like this:** "Before I put any options together, can I ask a few things? How long are you planning to stay here? And if you could change one thing about the plumbing, what would it be?"

## Diagnose the person: how the problem affects the people in the house

You've diagnosed the hot water unit. Now **diagnose the person**: how is this problem affecting the people who live here?

The same failing unit means something different in every home:

- for a family with teenagers, it's the cold-shower arguments every morning
- for a shift worker, it's no hot water when they get home
- for an older couple, it's the worry of a leak while they're away

That's what they're really buying a fix for. **Customers buy peace of mind, not parts.** Nobody wants a tempering valve. They want to stop worrying about someone getting scalded.

The distance between "a new unit" and "never thinking about the hot water again" is the **value gap**. It closes when the customer sees what the fix means for them. So ask, listen, and use their words when you present.

> **Say it like this:** "You mentioned the pressure's been a problem since you moved in. What has that meant for you, day to day?"

> **On the job:** In Concord, the owner mentions that her elderly mother is moving in next month. That one detail changes the conversation: a tempering valve so the hot water can't scald, and a system nobody has to think about. Present it in those terms.

## Consequence questions and the cost of doing nothing: today's planned fix, water damage in three months, the 2am emergency at after-hours rates

**The leak doesn't pause while they save up.** Doing nothing has a cost too. Lay it out honestly:

- **Today:** a planned fix, at today's price.
- **In three months:** water damage, and a bigger bill.
- **The worst night:** a 2am emergency call-out, at after-hours rates.

Don't just tell them. Ask. **Consequence questions** let the customer state the cost of waiting in their own words, which lands far harder than hearing it from you:

- "What happens if it lets go while you're away?"
- "If we leave it, what do you think it looks like by winter?"

**Consequences, not scare tactics.** Describe what really happens if they leave it, calmly and honestly. Never exaggerate, never use words like "illegal" or "dangerous" to push a decision, and if it could genuinely hold for a while, say so.

> **Say it like this:** "Honestly, what happens if we leave it is the part I'd worry about."

> **On the job:** In North Rocks, the owners say they'll "sort the hot water next year". You ask what happens if it goes while the family's staying over at Christmas. They answer it themselves, and the conversation changes.
