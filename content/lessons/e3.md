## We never charge by the hour, but every estimate is built on hours

Customers never pay us by the hour. They get a **fixed upfront price** from the pricebook or an approved template, and it doesn't change whether the job takes one hour or three.

But behind every fixed price is an estimate of **hours and materials**. Someone worked out how long the job should take and what it needs. The price only works for the business if the job comes in close to that estimate.

That's why the hours on your estimate matter so much:

- they set the installer's target, and their efficiency pay depends on beating it
- they decide whether the job makes the profit it was priced for
- when they're wrong, the customer doesn't pay for the extra time. **We do.**

So **never improvise a price.** Use the pricebook and templates. Pricebook prices are built to hold the business's guardrails. A price made up at the kitchen table isn't.

> **On the job:** You quote a shower mixer swap in Maroubra on the template hours, without noticing the old mixer is set deep into a tiled wall. The job takes twice as long as the estimate. The customer pays the fixed price, as promised. So who paid for the extra time, and what could you have done at the quote?

> **Say it like this:** "We don't charge by the hour. You'll get a fixed price before we start, so whether it takes me one hour or three, the price doesn't change."

## The price split: labour and materials, running the business, profit

Every price splits roughly three ways:

- **About 50% labour and materials.** The people doing the work and the parts they use.
- **About 30% running the business.** Vans, the office, dispatch, insurance, admin and everything else that gets you to the door.
- **About 20% profit.** What's left.

These are the business's guardrails: **50% gross profit**, which is what's left after labour and materials, and **20% net profit**, which is what's left once the business has been run as well.

Customers tend to think a price is "parts plus an hour". So do some techs. But the customer only sees you, for the time you're there. They don't see the van that got you there, the office that took the call, dispatch, the insurance or the lifetime labour warranty on your work. All of it has to come out of the price.

And the profit isn't spare money. It's what keeps the business healthy enough to keep investing in the team.

> **On the job:** A customer in Strathfield watches you replace a tap in under an hour and says, "That's a lot for an hour's work." What they saw was the hour. What the price also paid for was the booking, the van, the parts on board, the office behind you and the warranty on the job.

## Worked example: a $1,000 job

Here's the split on a **$1,000 job**:

- **About $500** pays for labour and materials.
- **About $300** runs the business.
- **About $200** is profit.

Look at how small that last number is. By the time the work is done and the business is run, **$800 of the $1,000 is already spent.** The profit is the thin slice on top.

That's why the small things matter so much. A forgotten part, an extra hour, a discount: none of them make the $800 any smaller. The labour, the materials and the running costs are the same either way. **Every leak comes out of the $200.**

Your sign-off for this module is walking through this $1,000 example and the discount maths in the next lesson **without notes.** Practise it out loud until it's second nature. If you can explain it simply, you understand it well enough to protect it.

> **Say it like this:** "On a $1,000 job, about $500 is labour and materials, about $300 runs the business, and about $200 is profit. Everything that goes wrong comes out of the $200."

> **On the job:** You finish a $1,000 job in Caringbah an hour over the estimate, after a trip to the supplier for a part you forgot. The customer pays $1,000, as quoted. The labour and the parts still cost what they cost, and now the extra hour has to come from somewhere. It comes from the $200.

## Why a 10% discount throws away half the profit

Take 10% off that $1,000 job and you've taken **$100.** It feels small. It isn't.

The work doesn't get smaller. Same parts, same hours, same van. So the $100 comes straight out of the $200 profit. **A 10% discount throws away half the profit** on the job.

Put another way: you'd need to do **two** discounted jobs to make the profit of **one** full-price job. Twice the work for the same result.

That's why **we sell on value, never on price.** Competitors compete on price and speed. We compete on trust and quality.

**Techs can't discount or change a price.** Only Harriet (Sales Manager) or Sadie (Office Manager) can. Never offer to "ask the office for a better price" in front of a customer: that's a discount by another name.

If the customer wants it cheaper, **step down an option.** Cheaper means fewer lines, never the same work for less. Never discount for free: if the price comes down, something comes out of the job. Otherwise they learn our first number was fake, and they'll always push.

> **Say it like this:** "I can't change the price, but I can show you a lower option. If we do just the urgent part today, here's where we land."

## Unestimated hours and unbilled materials

Discounts are the leak you can see. These two are the ones you can't.

**Every unestimated hour comes straight out of the profit.** They usually come from:

- an estimate that didn't allow for the real site: tight access, old pipework, stairs or roof spaces
- a forgotten part and a supply run mid-job
- rework or a return trip
- extra work done without being re-priced

**Unbilled materials** are parts we paid for but didn't charge for. Every material you use should be added to the job from the pricebook as you use it. A part that isn't recorded is profit given away.

Extra work done "as a favour" is both at once. If it turns up on the invoice later, it breaks the no-hidden-costs promise. If it doesn't, the business pays for it.

What you can do:

1. Estimate realistically, and note anything that will add time in the scope.
2. Carry the right parts, and plan pickups before the job.
3. Record every material from the pricebook as you use it.
4. If the job changes, stop, show the customer, price it from the pricebook and get signed approval before you carry on.

> **On the job:** Halfway through a job in Engadine, the old isolation valve won't turn off. You could quietly fit one from the van. Instead, you stop, show the customer, price the new valve from the pricebook and get their signed approval. No surprises on the invoice, and nothing given away.
