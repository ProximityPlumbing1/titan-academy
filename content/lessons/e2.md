## Which templates exist and why they keep pricing and wording consistent

An estimate template is a ready-made quote for a job we do often. It holds the pricebook lines, the standard scope wording, the inclusions and exclusions, the materials and the estimated hours.

**Templates mean every customer gets the same price and the same wording for the same job.** That matters more than it sounds:

- **Trust.** Neighbours compare quotes. Property managers compare jobs across their rentals. If the same job comes out at two different prices, both customers wonder which one was real.
- **Accuracy.** The lines, materials and hours have already been thought through, so nothing gets missed.
- **Speed.** Building the quote at the kitchen table is much faster than starting from scratch.
- **A clean handover.** The installer reads wording they already know, so they know exactly what was sold.

The five standard bundles are being built as templates: **Hot Water, Whole-Home Water Protection, Bathroom Refresh, Drain Fix-for-Good and Landlord Compliance Pack.** Each one sets out Essential, Standard, Premium and Elite, with Elite presented first. [CONFIRM: the current template list]

You'll find templates in the ServiceTitan app, alongside the pricebook.

Same unit, same job, different wording and a different total. The neighbours compare quotes over the fence. What does that do to their trust in both of us?

## Always check for an existing template first

Before you build anything, **search for a template.** Never build from scratch when a template exists.

A quote built from scratch is where the mistakes creep in:

- a line or a material gets missed
- the hours are a guess instead of a tested estimate
- the wording is vague, and the installer has to call you to understand it
- it takes longer, while the customer waits at the table

Start from the template, then make it fit this home. Add the pricebook lines your findings support, because the bundling rule still applies: never quote one job when the site shows more. Note anything specific to the site in the scope, like tight access or old pipework. **Never change a template's price or type one in.**

If no template fits, build the quote from pricebook lines, with every line from the pricebook. If you find yourself building the same thing again and again, it should be a template. That's the next lesson.

If a template's wording or hours look wrong for the job, don't quietly work around it. Raise it with Harriet (Sales Manager) so it gets fixed for everyone.

> **On the job:** At a kitchen table in Dee Why, your camera shows roots in the drain. Instead of typing lines one by one, you open the Drain Fix-for-Good template and build the four options from what the footage shows.

## Building a template: standard scope wording, inclusions, exclusions, materials and estimated hours

**New templates are for job types we quote repeatedly.** A one-off job doesn't need one. If you've quoted the same job or bundle twice, it probably should be a template.

A good template has five parts:

1. **Standard scope wording.** Plain English the customer and the installer can both follow.
2. **Inclusions.** Everything we'll do.
3. **Exclusions.** Everything we won't do, like making good the plaster or tiling after access.
4. **Materials.** Every part the job needs, each one a pricebook line.
5. **Estimated hours.** Realistic hours for how the installer will actually do the job. For a bundle, the combined hours have to reflect how a bigger job is really paced.

**Every line comes from the pricebook.** A template has no discount lines and no price overrides. If a part you need isn't in the pricebook, flag it so it can be added. Never type in your own price.

For a bundle, set out all four options. Cheaper options have fewer lines, never lower prices.

Before you send it for review, apply the handover test: could an installer read this template and do the job without ever calling you?

> **On the job:** You've quoted new cistern internals and new flexi hoses at three homes in Sutherland this month, each from scratch. You draft it as a template with the scope wording, what's in and out, the parts, and the hours it actually took you.

## Review and sign-off before a template goes live

**Every new template is reviewed and signed off before it goes live.** [CONFIRM: who approves templates]

Send your draft to Harriet (Sales Manager) for review. Don't use it on customers, and don't share it around the team, until it's approved.

Review matters because a template gets copied onto every quote for that job. One wrong hour estimate or a missing exclusion isn't one bad quote. It's every quote, until someone notices.

The review checks:

- **Price:** every line from the pricebook, with no discount or override lines.
- **Wording:** plain English, with no jargon.
- **Inclusions and exclusions:** both clearly stated.
- **Materials:** nothing missing.
- **Hours:** realistic for the installer.

If it comes back with changes, that's the system working. Fix them and send it again.

When the pricebook or the templates change, you'll get a refresher on this module, so the way you quote keeps up.

Your sign-off for this module is a draft template that passes review.

> **On the job:** Your Sutherland cistern template comes back from review. The exclusions don't say the cistern and pan themselves aren't being replaced, so a customer could expect a new toilet. You add that in plain English and resend it. Next time anyone quotes that job, the customer knows exactly what they're getting.
