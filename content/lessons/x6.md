## Touchpoint 1, during the inspection before any price: 'Whatever we find, we've got ways to make it easy, including spreading it out if you'd ever want'

Plenty of experienced sellers only raise finance when the customer flinches. By then it sounds like a rescue. So on every big job we **seed it three times**: during the inspection, while you walk through the options, and after the price and the silence. Touchpoint 1 happens **during the inspection, before there's any price on the table**. They're not deciding anything yet, so the idea goes in with zero pressure.

**It's a throwaway line, never a pitch.** Same words, same tone, to every customer on every big job. Say it only to the people who look worried and you're prejudging again.

In the US data quoted in the October session, 41% of consumers actively look for financing, yet homeowners rarely ask for it. **If you don't raise it, nobody will.**

> **Say it like this:** "Whatever we find, we've got ways to make it easy, including spreading it out if you'd ever want."

> **On the job:** You're tracing a leak in the roof space of a big Castle Hill home, with the owner at the bottom of the ladder. Two new European cars in the driveway. Nothing's priced yet, and you'd normally never mention finance at a house like this. Do you drop the line now? What stops you raising it this early?

## Touchpoint 2, while walking through options, tied to Elite: 'For the bigger one, some customers just pay it weekly rather than all at once'

Touchpoint 2 comes **while you're walking them through the options**, and it's tied to **Elite** specifically.

You've led with Elite, the "do it properly once" option. It's the biggest number they'll hear today, so that's where the line goes. **The big job feels reachable before they've even seen the total.**

Keep it light. One sentence, then straight back to the options. If it turns into a speech about how finance works, it's become a pitch.

The October session quoted ServiceTitan's US customer analysis: contractors using integrated financing had a 16% higher average ticket than those who don't. That's a correlation, not a promise. The point is simpler: if the top option feels out of reach, nobody chooses it.

> **Say it like this:** "For the bigger one, some customers just pay it weekly rather than all at once."

> **On the job:** In a Pennant Hills bathroom, you open with Elite: new tapware, a new toilet suite, isolation valves and a Care Plan. As you finish Elite, you add the line and move straight on to Premium. Nobody asks about it yet. That's fine. The seed is planted, and you'll bring it home after the price.

*Source: "Selling the Big Job", chapter 6. The 16% figure is ServiceTitan customer analysis (US): correlation, not a guarantee.*

## Touchpoint 3, after the price and the silence: 'Like I mentioned, I can turn that into a weekly figure in about two minutes'

Touchpoint 3 comes **after the number, after the silence**. Scope. Price. Silence. You say the price, then stop talking and let them speak first.

Don't jump to finance to fill the quiet. Offered into your own silence, it sounds like you're saving the sale. The silence feels long to you, not to them.

When they do speak, and it's a flinch, you bring finance home. **It's not a new ask.** They've already heard it twice, so it sounds like the next step, not a lifeline. Often they raise it themselves, and then it's their idea.

The session quoted US HVAC data: when homeowners used financing rather than cash or card, the closing ratio was 12% higher and the average ticket 13% higher. Again, correlation, not a guarantee.

> **Say it like this:** "Like I mentioned, I can turn that into a weekly figure in about two minutes."

> **On the job:** You've stated $14,800 for the Premium option in a Carlingford home. They go silent and look at each other. You hold it. Then one of them says, "That's… a lot in one hit." You don't justify it and you don't discount. You say the line, open HomePro and get the real weekly figure.

*Source: "Selling the Big Job", chapters 8 and 9. The 12% and 13% figures are Synchrony HVAC data via ACCA (US): correlation, not a guarantee.*

## Who wants finance: cash-is-king (keeps money invested), cashflow smoothers, and the budget-constrained. Finance means savvy, not broke

Most of us picture one kind of finance customer: the one who can't pay. That's only one of three. The October session named the same three types that lenders target:

- **Cash is king.** Often the wealthiest. They could pay outright, but they'd rather keep their money invested or liquid.
- **The cashflow smoother.** A solid earner who doesn't want a 30 grand hit in one month.
- **The budget-constrained.** They genuinely couldn't do the job otherwise. Finance is why the sale happens.

**Finance means savvy, not broke.** Two of the three aren't short of money at all. They just have better things to do with it than spend it on plumbing in one go.

If you only think of the third type, you'll only offer finance to people who look like they need it. That's prejudging, and it costs you the other two. The session's US data showed 65% of Americans have under $1,000 set aside for home repairs, and you can't tell who's who from the driveway.

> **On the job:** A retired couple in Wahroonga choose Elite for a re-pipe. They could write the cheque, but they'd rather leave their savings where they are and spread it out. They're cash-is-king, and you'd never have known if you hadn't offered.

*Source: "Selling the Big Job", chapter 10. The 65% figure is Synchrony data via ACCA (US).*

## Offer it to everyone with a flattering reason. Offered upfront it's a feature; offered after 'too expensive' it's a lifeline

**Make finance standard, not special.** Offer it to everyone, casually, on every big job. Offered only to the person who looks like they need it, it becomes shameful. So give a **flattering reason**: make it sound like what sensible people do, because often it is.

Timing changes how it lands:

- **Offered upfront, it's a feature.** Part of how we work, like the fixed price.
- **Offered after "too expensive", it's a lifeline.** It sounds like you're saving a sale, and the customer feels it.

That's why the three touchpoints start before there's a price on the table.

**The benchmark is 40%.** If fewer than 40% of big jobs are financed, it isn't being offered consistently. Your sign-off is finance offered on every big job for two weeks, checked against HomePro applications.

In the session's case study, one US contractor led with finance on every quote and its financed share of deals went from 7% to 80%, with average ticket up 62%. That's one company's result, not a promise.

> **Say it like this:** "A lot of our customers prefer to keep their cash free and just spread this out."

*Source: "Selling the Big Job", chapters 10 and 11. The case study is Momnt's Braga Brothers study, and the 40% benchmark comes from ServiceTitan's financing guidance (both US): correlation, not a guarantee.*

## The HomePro flow: Option → Apply → Approved → Book → Finish. HomePro sits outside ServiceTitan, so the proposal shows the full scope and real price and never mentions finance

**Lead with the solution. Finance removes the barrier.** It's the "how", not the "what". The customer chooses the right option for their home first, and finance is how they pay for it.

Know the five steps well enough to walk them from memory on a live job. That's your sign-off.

1. **Option**: the customer chooses the option. Finance never decides what goes in it.
2. **Apply**: they apply through HomePro.
3. **Approved**: the application comes back approved.
4. **Book**: the job is booked.
5. **Finish**: the job is completed.

## Quoting weekly figures: always use HomePro's real numbers, never an illustration

Payment options are the real unlock. **Most people don't have a lump sum sitting there. Almost everyone has a weekly number.** So we talk in weekly figures, before they ask.

The October Masterclass made the point with $2,000 that scares them against about $40 a week they can say yes to. That figure was labelled as an illustration: $2,000 over 12 months, before interest and fees. It made the point. It's not for quoting.

**On the job, always use HomePro's real numbers, never an illustration.** That's what "about two minutes" is for: getting the real figure for this customer and this option. Never:

- make up a rough number, like "about forty bucks a week"
- promise it's interest-free
- say they'll be approved, or guess at rates, fees or terms
